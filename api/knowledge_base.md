# AQI category: Good (0-50)
Air quality is considered satisfactory. Outdoor activity is safe for everyone, including children, older adults, and people with respiratory conditions like asthma.

# AQI category: Moderate (51-100)
Air quality is acceptable. Unusually sensitive individuals should consider limiting prolonged outdoor exertion. Most people can continue normal outdoor activity.

# AQI category: Unhealthy for sensitive groups (101-150)
Children, older adults, pregnant people, and those with asthma, heart disease, or other respiratory conditions may start to experience health effects. These groups should reduce prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion. The general public is not likely to be affected.

# AQI category: Unhealthy (151-200)
Everyone may begin to experience health effects such as throat irritation, coughing, or shortness of breath. Sensitive groups should avoid outdoor exertion entirely. The general public should limit prolonged outdoor activity, especially strenuous exercise.

# AQI category: Very unhealthy (201-300)
Health alert: the risk of health effects is increased for everyone. Sensitive groups should stay indoors and keep activity levels low. The general public should avoid outdoor exertion and consider staying indoors with windows closed.

# AQI category: Hazardous (301+)
Health warning of emergency conditions. The entire population is more likely to be affected. Everyone should avoid all outdoor physical activity. Sensitive groups should remain indoors with air purification if available.

# Guidance for children and infants
Children breathe faster than adults relative to their body size and spend more time outdoors, so they inhale proportionally more pollution. On unhealthy or worse days, keep outdoor play indoors, close windows during peak traffic hours (typically early morning and evening), and avoid outdoor sports practice.

# Guidance for people with asthma or respiratory conditions
Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) can trigger airway inflammation and asthma attacks. Keep rescue inhalers accessible, avoid outdoor exertion when AQI is above 100, and consider an N95-rated mask if going outside is unavoidable during unhealthy conditions.

# Guidance for pregnant people
Exposure to elevated PM2.5 during pregnancy has been associated with adverse outcomes in research literature. Pregnant individuals are advised to follow the same precautions as sensitive groups from the "unhealthy for sensitive groups" threshold upward.

# Guidance for older adults
Older adults are more likely to have underlying heart or lung conditions that are aggravated by air pollution. Limit outdoor exertion above AQI 100, and monitor for symptoms like unusual fatigue or shortness of breath.

# About PM2.5
PM2.5 refers to fine particulate matter smaller than 2.5 micrometers in diameter, small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream. It is one of the most consistently harmful air pollutants tracked worldwide and the primary driver of Kathmandu Valley's poor air quality, especially during the dry season (November to March) due to vehicle emissions, brick kilns, and open burning.

# About wind and dispersion
Low wind speed allows pollution to accumulate rather than disperse, which is why AQI often spikes during calm mornings and evenings in valley terrain like Kathmandu, where surrounding hills trap pollutants.

# Seasonal pattern in Kathmandu Valley
Air quality in the Kathmandu Valley is typically worst from November through March (dry, low-wind winter season) and improves during the monsoon (June to September) when rain washes particulates out of the air.

# General outdoor exercise recommendation
For outdoor exercise, early morning is not always the safest time in Kathmandu Valley -- pollution can be trapped near the ground overnight. Checking the current AQI before heading out is more reliable than assuming a time of day is safe.
